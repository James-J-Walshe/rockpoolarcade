The research behind the games

Caught in the same net

A ray, a turtle, a shark — and a tiny porpoise. They look totally different, but all four are in danger of disappearing from the oceans forever, and mostly for the same reasons. Here's their story.

The four problems they all share

🎣

Fishing nets

Saws, shells, hammer-shaped heads — even little porpoises — all get tangled in fishing nets really easily. Most of the time, nobody was even trying to catch them. They just got stuck. Scientists call this bycatch.

💰

Hunted for parts

People pay a lot of money for fins, saws and patterned shells. Selling these parts between countries is against the law now — but it still happens in secret.

🏖️

Losing their homes

They grow up in shallow water near the coast — mangrove forests, coral reefs and quiet beaches. People keep building over exactly these places.

⌛

Slow to grow up

They take many years to become adults and don't have many babies. When lots are killed, it takes a very, very long time for their numbers to come back.

Meet the four

🦈

Sawfish · all 5 species endangered

Yes, it's a ray — a flat cousin of sharks! Its "saw" is a long nose edged with teeth: brilliant for hunting fish, but it tangles in nets almost instantly, even old lost ones drifting in the sea. Baby sawfish grow up hiding among mangrove roots, and a sawfish isn't fully grown until it's about 8–10 years old. They've vanished from over half the places they used to live — their last big strongholds are northern Australia and Florida.

🐢

Hawksbill sea turtle · critically endangered

Named for its beak, shaped like a hawk's. Turtles have to swim up for air — if one gets tangled in a net underwater, it can't reach the surface to breathe. For hundreds of years its beautiful patterned shell was made into jewellery and combs (that's what "tortoiseshell" actually was). A hawksbill can't have babies until it's 20–30 years old — older than a lot of teachers have been teaching!

🦈

Scalloped hammerhead shark · critically endangered

Its wide head is packed with super-senses for finding prey — and it's terrible news around fishing nets, where it gets stuck just like a sawfish's saw. Its fins are among the most expensive in the shark fin trade, and hammerheads swim in huge schools, so one boat in the wrong place can catch a whole group at once.

🐬

Vaquita · only about 10 left

The world's smallest porpoise — and its rarest sea mammal. Nobody hunts vaquitas: the gillnets that drown them are set illegally for a fish called the totoaba, whose swim bladder sells for huge money. The vaquita is just in the wrong place, and it lives in only one place on the whole planet — the very top of Mexico's Gulf of California. A mother has one baby every one or two years, so every single loss matters.

The good news

We know exactly what the problems are, and protecting these animals works. Where nets are banned and nursery homes are kept safe — northern Australia, Florida, guarded turtle beaches — the animals hang on. The vaquita is the starkest warning: with only about 10 left, its future depends on getting every last illegal net out of its small corner of the sea. Saving them isn't a mystery. It's a choice.

What you can do

  • Look for the sustainable seafood label when your family shops
  • Keep balloons and plastic bags out of the sea — turtles mistake them for jellyfish
  • Never buy tortoiseshell, shark fin or sawfish souvenirs
  • Tell someone the vaquita's story — most people have never heard of it
